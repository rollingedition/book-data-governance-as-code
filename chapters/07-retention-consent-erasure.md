# Retention, consent and erasure

By the end of this chapter you can keep retention rules as reviewed config, make a withdrawal of consent change the next query, and erase one person from every table their data reached. You'll apply the rules against a day you pass in, find those tables from lineage, and keep only what your legal team has recorded an exception for. You'll also remove what a row delete leaves behind in an Iceberg table, old snapshots, unreferenced files and column statistics, and finish with a scan that reads every file left and shows that none of them holds the person.

An online learning platform, which sells professional courses to adult learners, has kept everything since its first sign-up in January 2024 in one Iceberg catalog: every support ticket, and every learning event with the IP address it came from. Nothing says how long either should stay. Two requests from one learner test the rest of its governance. Learner 3590 withdraws their consent to marketing email, and some weeks later asks the platform to erase their data. The first should change what marketing sees from the next query on. The second should leave no copy of the learner anywhere: not in the tables built from theirs, and not in the old versions of each table that Iceberg keeps on purpose.

Three terms name the chapter's subjects. **Retention** is how long the platform keeps each kind of data. **Consent** is a person's agreement to one use of their data, such as marketing email, which they can withdraw. **Erasure** is removing a person's data on request. The chapter adds two things to the book's shared code: the platform's retention rules, in `governance/retention.toml`, and the module `lib.retention`, which applies them, plans and carries out an erasure, cleans up and checks the files afterwards, and keeps the register of erasures. It uses `lib.lineage` from [chapter 4](#lineage-from-pipelines) to find where a learner's data went, and the classifications in `lib.metadata` from [chapter 2](#a-catalog-you-can-test). Every example runs PyIceberg {{version:pyiceberg}} on a local catalog.

## Keep retention rules in a reviewed file {#retention-rules-in-a-file}

A retention rule says how long one kind of data is kept, from which date the time counts, and which rows it applies to. The EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) states the principle as storage limitation: personal data are to be "kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data are processed" (Article 5(1)(e)) ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)). Its Recital 39 asks the controller to set time limits for erasure or for a periodic review ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)). NIST's catalog of security and privacy controls describes the same job in neutral words, asking that information be disposed of, destroyed or erased once its retention period ends (control SI-12(3)) ([NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/53/r5/upd1/final)). The platform's owners and lawyers decide how long is necessary; the code applies what they decide, the same way every time.

That argues for rules as config, reviewed like the access policies, and for code that applies them to a day it is given, not the clock. A job that reads the clock deletes different rows depending on when it runs: a test can't pin its result, and a rerun after a failure can delete more than the run it replaces.

The platform's rules are in `governance/retention.toml`. The periods are the platform's own choices for this book's case, not legal advice:

```toml
[tables."support.tickets"]
keep_days = 730
counted_from = "opened_at"        # tickets record when they were opened, not when they were closed
only_when = { column = "status", equals = "closed" }

[tables."learning.events"]
keep_days = 365
counted_from = "occurred_at"

[tables."billing.payments"]
keep_days = 3650                  # a placeholder for the period the platform's legal team sets for payment records
counted_from = "paid_at"
```

Closed tickets are kept two years, events one year, and payments 3,650 days, a placeholder that [the erasure plan](#plan-erasure-from-lineage) relies on. The tickets rule should count from when a ticket was closed, but the table records only `opened_at`, so a ticket opened 25 months ago and closed last week goes at once. The platform lives with that because adding a `closed_at` column is the support team's change to their own table, not the governance team's; until they make it, the rule errs toward deleting early. `lib.retention.due` turns a rule and a day into an Iceberg filter; the example counts the rows it selects on the book's "today", Oct 1, 2026, and 90 days later:

[Example: retention-rules-due](../examples/retention-rules-due.py)

The rows due depend on the day passed in and on the data, and on nothing else. On Oct 1, 2026, the events rule makes {{out:retention-rules-due:learning.events on 2026-10-01, rows due}} events due, and 90 days later it makes {{out:retention-rules-due:learning.events on 2026-12-30, rows due}}. The tickets rule makes {{out:retention-rules-due:support.tickets on 2026-10-01, rows due}} closed tickets, all opened more than two years earlier, due on Oct 1, and {{out:retention-rules-due:support.tickets on 2026-12-30, rows due}} on Dec 30. The output gives the table sizes, and the payments rule's counts too.

**Working rule:** Keep each table's retention period, the column it counts from and the state a row must be in, in a reviewed file, and apply it against a day the scheduler passes in, never the clock. Count from the date the rule means. When the table doesn't record that date, as the platform's tickets don't record when they closed, ask the table's owner to add the column rather than let the rule count from another date for good, and say in the rule's file which way it errs until then.

## Delete expired rows with row deletes {#delete-expired-rows}

With [the rules in a file](#retention-rules-in-a-file), the retention job deletes what they say. Iceberg writers delete rows in one of two ways, set by the table property `write.delete.mode`, which defaults to `copy-on-write` ([Iceberg configuration](https://iceberg.apache.org/docs/latest/configuration/)). Copy-on-write rewrites each affected data file without the deleted rows. Merge-on-read leaves the data file alone and writes a delete file beside it. The spec has two kinds of delete file: position deletes, which mark a row by its data file and its position (a deletion vector, in tables of version 3 or above), and equality deletes, which mark it by the values of one or more columns ([Iceberg table spec](https://iceberg.apache.org/spec/)). PyIceberg {{version:pyiceberg}}'s `Table.delete` always copies on write: with `merge-on-read` set, it warns that merge-on-read isn't supported yet, and rewrites the files anyway ([source](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-python/blob/pyiceberg-0.12.0/pyiceberg/table/__init__.py)). Either way, a delete is a commit, a new snapshot, and the snapshot before it is still there.

`lib.retention.apply` deletes, for each rule, the rows `due` returns for the day it's given, and records the rule's table and the day in the commit's snapshot summary, so the table's own history says why the rows went. Applying the rules for Oct 1, 2026 twice, then reading each table as it is now and by time travel, shows what a delete leaves behind, and expiring the old snapshots by age is the last step:

[Example: apply-retention](../examples/apply-retention.py)

A retried run is harmless: a second run for the same day deleted {{out:apply-retention:rows deleted by running again for the same day}} rows and made no commit. The first run deleted {{out:apply-retention:learning.events, rows deleted}} events and {{out:apply-retention:support.tickets, rows deleted}} tickets, and the output gives the payments rule's count. Each table it changed went from an `append` snapshot to an `overwrite`, and the summary shows what copy-on-write costs: to delete the old events, PyIceberg wrote the other {{out:apply-retention:learning.events, rows written again to delete the others}} again, because the table is one unpartitioned file. Partitioned by month of `occurred_at`, the table would let the same delete drop whole files instead, which `Table.delete` does when every row of a file matches. In PyIceberg {{version:pyiceberg}}, writing a table partitioned by month needs the `pyiceberg-core` extra, which this book's examples don't install.

A delete doesn't remove the rows from the past. Read as it is now, `learning.events` holds {{out:apply-retention:learning.events, rows due, read now}} rows past their retention, but read by time travel, the snapshot before the delete still returns {{out:apply-retention:learning.events, rows due, read by time travel}}, IP addresses included, and the Parquet file that holds them is still on disk. `lib.retention.expire_by_age` expires snapshots older than the table's own `history.expire.max-snapshot-age-ms`, five days here, measured on the clock. It expires {{out:apply-retention:learning.events, snapshots expired by age today}} snapshots of `learning.events` straight after the delete, and {{out:apply-retention:learning.events, snapshots expired by age six days later}} six days later. The retention job isn't finished until the old snapshots are [expired](#expire-snapshots) and [the files no snapshot references are removed](#remove-unreferenced-files): the same clean-up an erasure needs.

**Working rule:** Apply retention as Iceberg row deletes, one rule at a time, and record the rule and the day in each commit's snapshot summary. Make the job idempotent for a given day. Partition large tables by the column a rule counts from, so its deletes drop whole files instead of rewriting them; in PyIceberg, install its `pyiceberg-core` extra to write them. Count the job done only once the old snapshots are expired and their files removed.

## Read consent where the query runs {#consent-at-query-time}

[Retention](#retention-rules-in-a-file) removes rows by age. Consent changes who may use them, and withdrawing it isn't the same event as asking for erasure, so the platform's code treats them as two paths. Article 7(3) of the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) says: "The data subject shall have the right to withdraw his or her consent at any time. The withdrawal of consent shall not affect the lawfulness of processing based on consent before its withdrawal." ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)). Article 17(1)(b) of the same regulation makes a withdrawal one ground for erasure, but only where no other legal ground for the processing remains. Whether that applies to a given request is for the platform's lawyers. In the code, a withdrawal changes one attribute; an erasure deletes rows.

On the platform, consent is an attribute of the data that the access policy reads, as [chapter 5](#narrow-by-attribute) set it up: marketing's row filter keeps only learners whose `marketing_consent` is true, and the course assistant's keeps only the reviews whose authors' `ai_training_consent` is true. So a withdrawal needs no change to the policy. The CRM, the application where learners manage their accounts, syncs the new value into `crm.learners`, and the next query marketing runs, through the enforcement layer that [chapter 6](#enforcing-policies-on-queries) builds to rewrite every query, returns one learner fewer.

The code that follows withdraws learner 3590's marketing consent and reads marketing's view at three points: before, after, and once the marketing pipeline has run again.

[Example: consent-withdrawal](../examples/consent-withdrawal.py)

Withdrawing consent changes one attribute, and the next query returns one learner fewer: marketing sees {{out:consent-withdrawal:before, learners marketing sees}} learners before the withdrawal, learner 3590 among them, and {{out:consent-withdrawal:after the withdrawal, learners marketing sees}} after it, without learner 3590. Marketing's row filters stay `{{out:consent-withdrawal:marketing's row filters on crm.learners}}`, and `crm.learners` still stores {{out:consent-withdrawal:after the withdrawal, rows stored in crm.learners}} rows. Nothing was deleted; the learner's account, enrollments and history are as they were.

The copy is the problem. `marketing.audience`, the list a marketing pipeline builds each night, applies the consent filter when it's built, and the policy gives marketing that table with no consent filter of its own. After the withdrawal it still holds {{out:consent-withdrawal:after the withdrawal, learner 3590's rows in marketing.audience}} row for learner 3590. Until someone classifies the table's columns, the policy redacts them for every reader; once someone does, as [chapter 4](#classifications-from-lineage) proposes, a campaign sent from the table before its next build reaches someone who said no. Only once the pipeline runs again does the count fall to {{out:consent-withdrawal:after the audience is built again, learner 3590's rows in marketing.audience}}. A training corpus is built with a consent filter too, and [chapter 9](#withdrawn-after-the-build) checks each build against today's consent before training on it.

A true-or-false column also says too little: `ai_training_consent` is null for everyone who signed up before the platform asked, and false can mean refused or withdrawn. The Data Privacy Vocabulary, a W3C Community Group report rather than a standard, names the states a consent can be in, given, refused, withdrawn, expired and unknown among them ([DPV 2.3](https://w3c-cg.github.io/dpv/2.3/dpv/)).

**Working rule:** Keep consent where the query runs: in one table that the system the learner uses keeps current, read by the policy as a row filter, so a withdrawal changes the next query and deletes nothing. When the platform's lawyers decide that a withdrawal is also a ground for erasure, send it through [the erasure path](#plan-erasure-from-lineage) as well. Rebuild every table built with a consent filter whenever consent changes, or give its readers the same filter. Record each consent as a state and the time it changed, and derive the true-or-false attribute the policy reads from it, counting unknown as not given.

## Plan an erasure from lineage {#plan-erasure-from-lineage}

[A withdrawal](#consent-at-query-time) changes one attribute; an erasure has to find the person everywhere. Article 17(1) of the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) gives a person the right to have their data erased without undue delay, on the grounds it lists ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)). Which grounds apply is a legal question. The platform treats a request as reaching every copy, so its first job is a list, in two halves, of where the person's data is. The source tables that hold a learner's ID come from the catalog: [chapter 2](#classify-pseudonymous-ids) decided that every table with a `learner_id` is part of every erasure, whatever its classification. The tables built from them come from lineage: [chapter 4](#impact-of-a-column-change)'s walk of the column graph, run from each source table, reaches every table their rows shaped.

Some data stays. Article 17(3) of the same regulation sets out where erasure doesn't apply, to the extent that processing is necessary, among them for compliance with a legal obligation under Union or Member State law ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)). Whether an exception applies, to which table, and for how long is the legal team's decision, never the code's. The code gives that decision one place, `governance/retention.toml`, where each exception is written with its reason. The book's reason is a placeholder, because we decide no legal question:

```toml
[erasure.exceptions."billing.payments"]
reason = "PLACEHOLDER: a legal obligation to keep payment records, such as tax records. The legal team replaces this with the obligation and the law it comes from."
decided_by = "legal team"         # a role, never a person's name
erase_columns = ["card_number"]   # what the obligation doesn't need; an empty list keeps the whole row
```

An erasure keeps an excepted table's rows until the table's [retention rule](#retention-rules-in-a-file) deletes them, and erases the columns `erase_columns` lists from them at once: here the full card number, which the platform's metadata says "Should not be stored at all". `lib.retention.exceptions` refuses an exception with no reason, or one for a table no rule ends.

Finding a derived table isn't the same as finding the learner's rows in it. `lib.retention.plan` looks at the lineage edges into each table the walk reaches, for a column copied unchanged, an IDENTITY edge in OpenLineage's terms, from the learner's ID or from the ID of one of their rows, such as a review. If there is one, the plan deletes by that column. If there's none, the learner's rows only shaped the table, and the plan runs the table's job again once the sources are erased. The plan reads the platform as the request finds it, weeks after the withdrawal: `crm.learners` holds learner 3590's withdrawn consent, and the four pipelines of [chapter 4](#lineage-from-pipelines), which build the analytics, marketing and AI tables, have run since, with their lineage recorded.

`lib.retention.plan` builds the steps from that catalog, and the example prints one line per step:

[Example: erasure-plan](../examples/erasure-plan.py)

The steps are decided by columns and lineage, not by rows. The plan visits {{out:erasure-plan:tables in the plan}} tables, found by their `learner_id` column or by lineage, and keeps the learner's {{out:erasure-plan:the learner's rows in billing.payments}} payments under the exception. It lists two tables that hold none of the learner's rows: `support.tickets`, since learner 3590 never opened a ticket, and `marketing.audience`, which holds {{out:erasure-plan:the learner's rows in marketing.audience}} rows because the pipeline rebuilt it after the withdrawal. `ai.review_corpus` has no `learner_id` at all, but lineage says its `review_id` is an IDENTITY copy of `learning.reviews.review_id`, so the plan deletes the learner's {{out:erase-learner:learning.reviews, rows deleted}} review by that ID. `analytics.course_performance` holds only counts and averages per course, so the plan rebuilds it from the erased sources. Whether an average that once counted the learner is still their data is a legal question; rebuilding costs one pipeline run, so the code doesn't need the answer.

Text can name a person in rows the plan doesn't delete, such as another learner's ticket. The example searches every string column for the identifying values `governance/retention.toml` lists, and finds {{out:erasure-plan:rows the plan doesn't delete whose text names the learner}} such rows. The search matches the first 15 characters of each value, lowercased, anywhere in the text: here `{{out:erasure-plan:the learner's identifying values, as the scan searches for them}}`, printed with the phone number masked, cut to 15 for the reason [the final scan](#verify-the-erasure) explains. A name typed differently or a phone number without separators needs the detectors of [chapter 3](#finding-personal-data).

**Working rule:** Plan every erasure from the catalog and the lineage graph, not from a list someone maintains: every table with the person's ID column, then every table lineage says their rows reach. Delete from a derived table by a column that lineage shows is an unchanged copy of an ID, and rebuild the derived tables the person's rows only shaped. Keep exceptions in config, written by your legal team with their reasons, and let the plan read and show them; never let the code decide one. Run [chapter 4's check for tables no job's lineage accounts for](#job-nobody-instrumented) first, because the plan can't reach a copy the lineage doesn't show.

## Erase the rows the plan lists {#erase-the-rows}

[The plan](#plan-erasure-from-lineage) says where learner 3590's data is, and `lib.retention.erase` carries it out in the plan's order. It deletes each step's rows with `Table.delete`, sources first, writes the rows an exception keeps again with the exception's columns empty, and runs the job of each table the plan rebuilds once the sources are done. Every commit records the request's ID, `{{out:erase-learner:crm.learners, the commit records}}`, in its snapshot summary. Iceberg keeps a summary in the table's metadata files after its snapshot expires: all of them by default, or the last `write.metadata.previous-versions-max` (100 by default) once `write.metadata.delete-after-commit.enabled` is set ([Iceberg maintenance](https://iceberg.apache.org/docs/latest/maintenance/), [Iceberg configuration](https://iceberg.apache.org/docs/latest/configuration/)). So the summary holds only the request's ID, which [the erasure register](#erased-learner-comes-back) pairs with a keyed hash of the learner's ID, and never the learner's email.

Running `lib.retention.erase` on that plan, under one request ID, then reading two tables as they are now and by time travel, is the next step:

[Example: erase-learner](../examples/erase-learner.py)

The erasure deleted {{out:erase-learner:learning.events, rows deleted}} of the learner's events and {{out:erase-learner:learning.enrollments, rows deleted}} enrollments, and one row each from `{{out:erase-learner:tables one row was deleted from}}`. It deleted nothing from `support.tickets` and `marketing.audience`, which held none, and it rebuilt `analytics.course_performance`.

Outside the exception, the deletes take the learner out of the current tables. `billing.payments` keeps the learner's {{out:erase-learner:billing.payments, the learner's rows kept}} payments, with {{out:erase-learner:billing.payments, card numbers left in them}} card numbers left in them. Read now, `crm.learners` holds {{out:erase-learner:the learner's rows in crm.learners, read now}} rows for the learner. Read by time travel to the snapshot before the erasure, it returns {{out:erase-learner:the learner's rows in crm.learners, read by time travel}}, with name, email, phone and address, and `learning.events` returns all of its events the same way.

To delete one row of `crm.learners`, PyIceberg wrote the other {{out:erase-learner:crm.learners, rows written again to delete one}} into a new file, as copy-on-write does, and the old file, with the learner in it, belongs to the snapshot before.

Across the plan's tables, {{out:erase-learner:snapshots that still hold the rows the erasure deleted}} snapshots still hold the rows the erasure deleted, and {{out:erase-learner:of them current}} of them are current. That is what snapshots are for: a reader can go back to any of them, and a bad commit can be rolled back. For an erasure, it means the delete took the learner out of the table but not out of storage.

**Working rule:** Carry out an erasure as row deletes in the plan's order, and record only a request ID in each commit, never a value of the person's. Treat the delete as the first of three steps: the snapshots before it still hold the person, and anyone allowed to time travel can read them until they're [expired](#expire-snapshots).

## Expire snapshots by the table's own settings {#expire-snapshots}

[After the erasure's deletes](#erase-the-rows), learner 3590's rows live on in the snapshots before them. The Iceberg spec says snapshots expire so that the data files they removed or replaced can be physically deleted ([Iceberg table spec](https://iceberg.apache.org/spec/)). Each table sets how in its properties ([Iceberg configuration](https://iceberg.apache.org/docs/latest/configuration/)): `gc.enabled` allows garbage collection, such as expiring snapshots and removing orphan files; `history.expire.min-snapshots-to-keep` is how many snapshots to keep however old they are; and `history.expire.max-snapshot-age-ms` is the age at which the rest may go. In Iceberg's Java library, expiring snapshots regularly is what deletes the unused data files ([Iceberg maintenance](https://iceberg.apache.org/docs/latest/maintenance/)).

PyIceberg {{version:pyiceberg}} does less. Its `table.maintenance.expire_snapshots()` commits one change, which removes the snapshots from the table's metadata and deletes no file ([source](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-python/blob/pyiceberg-0.12.0/pyiceberg/table/update/snapshot.py)). Its documentation offers it to "clean up table metadata and reduce storage costs" ([PyIceberg API](https://py.iceberg.apache.org/api/)). Nothing in it reads `gc.enabled`, and only its command-line tool reads the `history.expire` settings. An issue asks for expiry to delete files too ([issue 2604](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-python/issues/2604)). The next example gives `crm.learners` {{out:expiry-ignores-table-settings:gc.enabled=false, snapshots}} snapshots, the load and two deletes, and expires them two ways: by age with PyIceberg, `older_than` the current time, and with `lib.retention.expire`, which reads the two settings first.

[Example: expiry-ignores-table-settings](../examples/expiry-ignores-table-settings.py)

PyIceberg's expiry ignores both settings. It left {{out:expiry-ignores-table-settings:gc.enabled=false, snapshots left by PyIceberg's expiry}} of the first table's {{out:expiry-ignores-table-settings:gc.enabled=false, snapshots}} snapshots, and {{out:expiry-ignores-table-settings:history.expire.min-snapshots-to-keep=3, snapshots left by PyIceberg's expiry}} of the second's. `older_than` expires every snapshot older than the time it's given except those a branch or tag points at, here only the current one.

The book's expiry refused the first table, `{{out:expiry-ignores-table-settings:gc.enabled=false, the book's expiry refuses}}`, and expired {{out:expiry-ignores-table-settings:history.expire.min-snapshots-to-keep=3, the book's expiry expires}} snapshots of the second. An owner sets these properties for reasons the expiring code can't see, and in PyIceberg they are only advice.

For an erasure, both settings mean the job can't finish: a table that keeps a snapshot from before the delete still holds the person. A job that stopped at that table would already have deleted rows from the tables before it and left the rest unexpired, so the check belongs before the first delete. `lib.retention.erase_everywhere` runs `lib.retention.blocked` on every table in the plan first: garbage collection off, more than one snapshot to keep, or a branch or tag besides `main`. If any table fails, the job refuses to start and names every such table.

A job that checks before it deletes should refuse when a setting would keep the learner. The code sets both settings and runs the job:

[Example: erasure-refuses-to-start](../examples/erasure-refuses-to-start.py)

Nothing was erased, because the job refused. It names both tables, `crm.learners` because `{{out:erasure-refuses-to-start:the job refuses, because of crm.learners}}`, and `learning.events` because `{{out:erasure-refuses-to-start:the job refuses, because of learning.events}}`, and {{out:erasure-refuses-to-start:tables the job changed}} tables changed.

The scan runs either way, from the traces the refusal carries, and finds the learner in {{out:erasure-refuses-to-start:files holding the learner}} files across {{out:erasure-refuses-to-start:tables whose files hold the learner}} tables. A person then decides with the tables' owners, rather than code overriding an owner's setting.

**Working rule:** Expire snapshots through one function of your own that reads `gc.enabled` and the `history.expire` settings before it expires anything, and refuses when garbage collection is off, because PyIceberg's expiry reads neither; check again at every PyIceberg release. Before an erasure deletes anything, check every table in its plan the same way, and refuse the whole erasure when one would keep the person. For an erasure, expire every snapshot older than the delete. For retention, expire by age, as `history.expire.max-snapshot-age-ms` says, measured on the clock, since snapshots carry real commit times.

## Remove the files no snapshot references {#remove-unreferenced-files}

Once [the old snapshots are expired](#expire-snapshots), the files only they referenced are unreferenced: under the table's location, but in no snapshot's metadata. Iceberg calls them orphans. Its Java library removes them in a separate action, and warns that removing orphan files with a retention interval shorter than the time any write might take to complete is dangerous; its default interval is 3 days ([Iceberg maintenance](https://iceberg.apache.org/docs/latest/maintenance/)). PyIceberg {{version:pyiceberg}} has no such action. Its release notes list none ([release notes](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-python/releases/tag/pyiceberg-0.12.0)). The issue that asked for one was closed by the project's stale bot, marked not planned ([issue 1200](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-python/issues/1200)). Two pull requests that added one were closed without being merged ([1958](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-python/pull/1958), [3358](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-python/pull/3358)). Its only call that deletes data files is `purge_table`, which deletes the whole table ([source](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-python/blob/pyiceberg-0.12.0/pyiceberg/catalog/__init__.py)).

So `lib.retention` removes them itself, from what the metadata tables PyIceberg exposes as `table.inspect` say a table references. `lib.retention.referenced` collects the metadata files, current and logged, every snapshot's manifest list, every manifest (a manifest lists data files, with each file's column statistics), every data and delete file live in any snapshot, and any statistics files. Every other file under the table's location is unreferenced, and `lib.retention.sweep` deletes the ones older than the minimum age its caller passes. `governance/retention.toml` sets that age to 72 hours, Java's default, and `lib.retention.min_file_age` reads it. After the erasure, the code expires the plan's tables, sweeps them with that age and then with none, and checks every file for the learner:

[Example: sweep-unreferenced-files](../examples/sweep-unreferenced-files.py)

Expiry removes snapshots, not files. Of the files under the plan's tables, {{out:sweep-unreferenced-files:files no metadata references}} are referenced by nothing, and {{out:sweep-unreferenced-files:files holding the learner, after expiry}} hold the learner. The sweep at the configured 72 hours deletes {{out:sweep-unreferenced-files:files swept with the configured minimum age}} unreferenced files, since every file was written seconds before, so the examples pass an age of zero, as one process writes their catalog. The sweep with no minimum age deletes all the unreferenced files, and {{out:sweep-unreferenced-files:files holding the learner, after the sweep}} file still holds the learner: a manifest that the current snapshot of `crm.learners` references.

When a file is deleted, its manifest entry stores the snapshot in which it was deleted and status 2 (deleted) ([Iceberg table spec](https://iceberg.apache.org/spec/)), and that entry keeps the deleted file's column statistics. By default, under the metrics mode `truncate(16)` ([Iceberg configuration](https://iceberg.apache.org/docs/latest/configuration/)), those include each string column's smallest value, cut to 16 characters, and its largest, cut to 16 with the last character raised by one, so that it still bounds every value in the file ([source](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-python/blob/pyiceberg-0.12.0/pyiceberg/utils/truncate.py)). The deleted entry in `crm.learners` keeps the email bounds `{{out:sweep-unreferenced-files:crm.learners, manifest entry with status 2, email bounds}}`. The first is learner 3590's email, typed in capitals, which sorts before every other email on the platform. The second is learner 4998's, the platform's largest, whose 16th character, an `m`, became an `n`. No learner is safe from this: each data file's entry keeps the smallest and largest value of every column, so a table of many small files names many learners.

**Working rule:** Remove unreferenced files after every expiry, with a sweep of your own built on the inspect tables, since PyIceberg has none, and keep a minimum age of days in production so that a write in progress keeps its files. Use an age of zero only where a single process writes. Don't count a person erased because no data file holds them: the manifests may still hold their values.

## Keep personal values out of manifests {#keep-values-out-of-manifests}

[The sweep](#remove-unreferenced-files) left the learner's email in a manifest, as a column bound. Iceberg lets a table choose, per column, what its manifests keep, with the property `write.metadata.metrics.column.<name>`: `none` keeps nothing, `counts` keeps value and null counts, `truncate(n)` adds bounds cut to n characters, and `full` adds whole bounds ([Iceberg configuration](https://iceberg.apache.org/docs/latest/configuration/)). PyIceberg {{version:pyiceberg}} looks the property up by the column's name when it writes a file ([source](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-python/blob/pyiceberg-0.12.0/pyiceberg/io/pyarrow.py)).

Typed by hand, those properties would drift from the classifications. `lib.retention.properties` generates them from `governance/metadata.toml` and the `[statistics]` section of `governance/retention.toml`: `none` for every column classified `personal` or `sensitive`, and for every column nobody has classified, the same fail-closed default the access policy uses. It adds the `history.expire` settings from the rules' `[snapshots]` section. `lib.retention.apply_properties` sets them on every table and rewrites each table's data once, because a metrics mode applies only to the files written after it's set; expiry and a sweep then remove the files from before. Learner 4998, whose email is the largest bound in the manifest of `crm.learners`, is the test case, erased on two platforms, with default statistics and with the generated properties.

[Example: statistics-off-for-personal-columns](../examples/statistics-off-for-personal-columns.py)

With statistics off for personal columns, the manifest keeps bounds for {{out:statistics-off-for-personal-columns:statistics off for protected columns, of them with bounds in the manifest}} of the table's {{out:statistics-off-for-personal-columns:statistics off for protected columns, crm.learners personal columns}} personal columns, and {{out:statistics-off-for-personal-columns:statistics off for protected columns, files holding learner 4998 after erasure, expiry and sweep}} files hold learner 4998 after the erasure, expiry and sweep.

With default statistics, the manifest keeps bounds for {{out:statistics-off-for-personal-columns:default statistics, of them with bounds in the manifest}} of the table's personal columns, and its email bounds are `{{out:statistics-off-for-personal-columns:default statistics, crm.learners email bounds}}`. After the erasure, expiry and sweep, {{out:statistics-off-for-personal-columns:default statistics, files holding learner 4998 after erasure, expiry and sweep}} file still holds learner 4998, through that raised upper bound.

The generated properties turn statistics off for {{out:statistics-off-for-personal-columns:columns with statistics turned off}} columns, {{out:statistics-off-for-personal-columns:of them in tables nobody has classified}} of them in `marketing.audience` and `ai.review_corpus`, the two tables that [chapter 2](#test-the-catalog-against-the-metadata) found with no metadata. `crm.learners` gets {{out:statistics-off-for-personal-columns:crm.learners, properties set}} properties: the two expiry settings, and `none` for each personal column.

The cost is pruning. Iceberg uses the bounds in manifests to skip files a query's filter can't match ([Iceberg table spec](https://iceberg.apache.org/spec/)), so without them, a query that filters on `email` reads every file. The platform filters and erases by `learner_id`, which is `internal` and keeps its statistics. `counts` is the other choice: it keeps the counts that help a planner, and no values. And because PyIceberg {{version:pyiceberg}} keeps the property under the column's old name on a rename, leaving the new name without it, the properties are generated again on every schema change, as [chapter 2](#key-column-metadata-by-field-id) asks of every view of the metadata keyed by name.

**Working rule:** Generate per-column metrics properties from your classifications, `none` or `counts` for every personal, sensitive or unclassified column, and apply them when a table is created or, for an existing table, with one rewrite followed by expiry and a sweep. Keep statistics on the ID columns you filter and erase by. Generate the properties again after every schema change.

## Prove the erasure by reading every file {#verify-the-erasure}

Each step so far removed something the one before left behind, and left something of its own: the [delete](#erase-the-rows), the expiry, the sweep and the [statistics settings](#keep-values-out-of-manifests). That is the case for checking the result rather than trusting the steps. The European Data Protection Board's report on how controllers handle erasure, adopted in February 2026, found backups, and anonymisation used in place of deletion, among the weak points, and recommends that controllers "Verify that erasure has been carried out and be able to demonstrate such erasure" ([EDPB, 2026](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-02/edpb_cef-report_2025_right-to-erasure_en.pdf)).

`lib.retention.scan` reads every file under the catalog's warehouse, each with its own format's reader, and looks for the learner's traces. `lib.retention.traces` notes those before the erasure: the IDs of every row the plan deletes, by column, and the first 15 characters, lowercased, of each identifying value. Fifteen, because a manifest keeps a largest value's first 15 characters unchanged and raises the 16th by one, so a search for 16 misses a person whose value is a file's largest, as learner 4998's email is. A Parquet file is read as rows. A manifest or manifest list, both Avro, is read record by record with PyIceberg's Avro reader: every string in it, bounds included, and the bounds of each ID column, decoded by the schema the manifest carries. A metadata file is read as text. In a table an exception keeps, the learner's IDs are expected, so there the scan looks only for values, including those of the columns the exception erased. `lib.retention.erase_everywhere` runs the whole job: plan, note the traces, check every table, erase, expire, sweep. The example below runs that job on the platform with the generated properties, then scans:

[Example: verify-erasure](../examples/verify-erasure.py)

The scan finds the learner in {{out:verify-erasure:files holding the learner}} of the {{out:verify-erasure:files read}} files it reads, and no reader can time travel to the learner, since {{out:verify-erasure:snapshots a reader can still time travel to}} older snapshots remain. The lines after the per-table counts are the evidence: the request's ID, the tables visited, the rows deleted, the table the job rebuilt, the table an exception keeps and the column erased from it, `{{out:verify-erasure:tables kept under an exception, columns erased}}`, with the reason in `governance/retention.toml`, and the scan's counts. They name no value of the learner's, so keeping them puts none back, which [chapter 8](#produce-evidence-as-data) relies on when it writes them to the audit log.

One line is a warning: `{{out:verify-erasure:tables missing their generated properties}}` lacks its generated properties, because `lib.pipelines.run` dropped the table and created it again without them. That table has no personal columns. The next table a pipeline rebuilds might have them, and the scan would then report its manifests' bounds as a failed erasure, so the check belongs after every pipeline run.

IDs can stay in a manifest too, since the ID columns keep their statistics. An erased ID that was a file's smallest or largest, such as learner 5000's, stays in the deleted entry's bounds, and the scan reports it. One more commit to the table, followed by an expiry and a sweep, removes it, because PyIceberg's next append leaves out a manifest that holds only deleted files ([source](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-python/blob/pyiceberg-0.12.0/pyiceberg/table/update/snapshot.py)).

The scan covers the warehouse only. Exports, backups, the source systems and logs outside it can hold the learner too, and the same traces can be searched for in each of them the platform can read.

**Working rule:** End every erasure with a scan of every file the platform stores, for the person's IDs and identifying values, read with each format's own reader, and keep its result, counts and IDs only, as the evidence. Search for as much of each value as every bound keeps unchanged, which is one character less than the truncation length. Treat any file that still holds the person as a failed erasure, not a warning. Search the stores outside the warehouse that you control, and list in the record the ones you can't search.

## What goes wrong in production {#retention-in-production}

Retention and erasure fail quietly: a delete that succeeds looks the same whether the data is gone or still in storage. The first failure here undoes a finished erasure.

### An erased learner comes back {#erased-learner-comes-back}

Besides the CRM's sync, the warehouse reloads all of `crm.learners` each night from the application's export, and the erasure ran in the warehouse only. Erasing learner 3590 everywhere [the plan](#plan-erasure-from-lineage) says, then running the nightly reload and the pipelines that read the table, shows the risk:

[Example: erased-learner-returns](../examples/erased-learner-returns.py)

The erasure does not last in the warehouse alone. After it, `crm.learners` holds {{out:erased-learner-returns:the learner's rows in crm.learners after the erasure}} rows for the learner; after the nightly run it holds {{out:erased-learner-returns:the learner's rows in crm.learners after the nightly run}} again, and `analytics.learner_activity`, rebuilt from it, holds {{out:erased-learner-returns:the learner's rows in analytics.learner_activity after the nightly run}}. `marketing.audience` stays clear only because the learner's consent comes back withdrawn and their enrollments, which it joins, were erased.

The check that catches this keeps an erasure register: for each request, its ID and a keyed hash (HMAC-SHA256) of the erased learner's ID, and nothing else. The key is the erasure job's secret, kept like the enforcement layer's pseudonymization key in [chapter 6](#write-masks-as-sql) but a different one, because whoever holds it can hash every learner ID and find whom the register names. After every load, restore and rebuild, `lib.retention.erased_rows` hashes each `learner_id` in every table with that column and looks it up, so a restore or a backfill is checked without the register holding anyone's ID. It finds the learner in `analytics.learner_activity` and `crm.learners`, and leaves out `billing.payments`, whose rows the exception keeps. The audit log's record of the erasure, in [chapter 8](#produce-evidence-as-data), holds the same request ID and counts, never the learner, so the register is the only place a request meets a learner, as a hash. We treat a keyed hash of an ID as pseudonymized data, never anonymized, so whether the platform may keep the register, and for how long, is a question for its lawyers.

**Working rule:** Erase in the source system first, or in the same request, and keep a register of keyed hashes of erased IDs that every load filters against and every run checks, with the key held as a secret apart from the data. Treat a row with an erased ID as an incident, as you would a leak.

### Other failures {#other-retention-failures}

- **Old snapshots and files keep "deleted" data.** A retention or erasure job that stops after its delete leaves everything readable by time travel, and in PyIceberg the files stay after expiry too. Run [the expiry](#expire-snapshots) and [the sweep](#remove-unreferenced-files) in the same job, then the scan.
- **Another engine deletes by merge-on-read.** PyIceberg reads the deletion vectors other engines write ([release notes](https://github.com/apache/iceberg-python/releases/tag/pyiceberg-0.12.0)). Rows deleted that way stay in their data files until compaction rewrites them, and a delete file may keep their values, since both kinds of delete file can encode the values of the rows they delete ([Iceberg table spec](https://iceberg.apache.org/spec/)). Compact the affected files before [the expiry](#expire-snapshots) and [the sweep](#remove-unreferenced-files), and scan the delete files too.
- **Backups keep everything the sweep deletes.** They are one of the weak points [the EDPB's report](#verify-the-erasure) names. Decide with the people who answer for the backups how they're erased or aged out, and record it with each request.
- **A derived table nobody erased.** A job that sends no lineage writes a copy the plan can't see, as [chapter 4's uninstrumented export](#job-nobody-instrumented) does. Run `lib.lineage.unaccounted` before every erasure.
- **A rebuilt table loses its properties.** A pipeline that drops and creates its table loses the generated statistics settings, and the next erasure leaves values in its manifests. Apply the properties after every run, or create tables with them.
- **A renamed column loses its setting.** In PyIceberg, the metrics property stays under the column's old name, so after a rename the new name has none. Generate the properties again on every schema change.
- **Retention counted from the wrong date.** A rule that means "after closing" but counts from opening deletes early, and one that counts from a load date deletes late. Store the date the rule means.

## What this chapter decided {#retention-decisions}

- **Retention rules are config,** applied against a day the job is given, never the clock. Events are kept 365 days, payments 3,650, and closed tickets 730 days from opening, because the table has no `closed_at`: the platform's choices, not legal advice.
- **Consent is an attribute the policy reads** at query time; a withdrawal deletes nothing, and tables built with a consent filter are rebuilt.
- **An erasure is planned from the catalog and the lineage:** every table with a `learner_id` column, then every table lineage reaches.
- **Exceptions to erasure are the legal team's,** recorded in `governance/retention.toml` with their reasons; the code keeps what they say until the table's retention rule ends it.
- **Commits record request IDs and rule names,** never a person's values.
- **The book's expiry reads each table's settings,** which PyIceberg's doesn't, and an erasure checks every table in its plan before it deletes anything.
- **The book's sweep removes unreferenced files,** with a minimum age of 72 hours in production.
- **Manifests keep no values of protected columns:** metrics mode `none`, generated from the classifications.
- **Every erasure ends with a scan** of every file, for IDs and 15 characters of each identifying value; its counts are the evidence.
- **The erasure register keeps a keyed hash of each erased ID,** checked after every load, restore and rebuild; whether to keep it, and how long, is for the lawyers.
